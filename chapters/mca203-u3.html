MCA 203 · Unit III

Knowledge Representation

Syllabus topics

First order predicate calculusSkolemizationResolution principle and unificationInference mechanismsHorn's clausesSemantic networksFrame systems and value inheritanceScriptsConceptual dependency

Overview

This unit shows how knowledge is represented so a program can reason with it: logic (predicate calculus, resolution, Horn clauses) and structured representations (semantic networks, frames, scripts, conceptual dependency). Resolution problems and conversion to clause form are common numerical-type questions.

Detailed notes

1. First-order predicate calculus (FOPL)

FOPL extends propositional logic with constants (ram), variables (x), functions (father(x)), predicates (man(x), loves(x,y)), connectives (∧ ∨ ¬ → ↔) and quantifiers: universal ∀ (“for all”) and existential ∃ (“there exists”). Examples: “All men are mortal” = ∀x man(x) → mortal(x); “Someone loves Ram” = ∃x loves(x, Ram). A well-formed formula (wff) follows these rules.

2. Conversion to clause form and Skolemization

  1. Eliminate → (replace P → Q by ¬P ∨ Q).
  2. Move ¬ inwards (De Morgan, ¬∀ = ∃¬).
  3. Standardise variable names apart.
  4. Skolemize: remove each existential quantifier.
  5. Move ∀ to the front (prenex) and drop them.
  6. Convert to conjunction of disjunctions (CNF) and split into separate clauses.

Skolemization: replace an existentially quantified variable by a Skolem constant (if no ∀ precedes it) or a Skolem function of the preceding universal variables. E.g. ∀x ∃y loves(x,y) becomes loves(x, f(x)); ∃y teacher(y) becomes teacher(c). It preserves satisfiability (not equivalence).

3. Resolution principle and unification

Resolution takes two clauses containing complementary literals and produces a resolvent: from (A ∨ B) and (¬B ∨ C) infer (A ∨ C). It is used by refutation: to prove goal G, add ¬G to the clauses and derive the empty clause (contradiction). Unification finds a substitution that makes two literals identical, e.g. P(x, f(y)) and P(a, f(b)) unify with {x/a, y/b}. The most general unifier (MGU) is the least restrictive one. An occurs check prevents x being unified with f(x).

4. Inference mechanisms

Modus ponens (P, P→Q ∴ Q), modus tollens, universal instantiation, forward chaining, backward chaining and resolution refutation. Properties: soundness (only valid conclusions) and completeness (all valid conclusions can be derived).

5. Horn clauses

A Horn clause has at most one positive literal: a rule (¬A ∨ ¬B ∨ C, i.e. A ∧ B → C), a fact (C) or a goal/query (¬A ∨ ¬B). Inference with Horn clauses is efficient (linear for propositional) and is the basis of Prolog (c :- a, b.).

6. Semantic networks

A graph: nodes are objects/concepts and labelled arcs are relations (is-a, instance-of, has-part, colour…). Supports property inheritance (Tweety is-a Bird, Bird can fly ⇒ Tweety can fly) and quick association; weak in representing quantifiers, negation and procedural knowledge, and has no standard semantics.

7. Frame systems and value inheritance

A frame represents a stereotyped object as a record of slots with facets: value, default, range, and attached procedures (if-needed, if-added). Frames form a hierarchy (class → subclass → instance); a slot value not found locally is inherited from a parent frame (default inheritance, with overriding). Used in expert systems and object-oriented design.

8. Scripts

A script (Schank) is a structured sequence of events for a stereotyped situation, e.g. the restaurant script. Components: entry conditions, props, roles, track, scenes (entering, ordering, eating, exiting) and results. Helps in story understanding and filling in missing events.

9. Conceptual dependency (CD)

Schank’s language-independent representation of meaning using primitive acts: ATRANS (transfer of possession), PTRANS (physical transfer), MTRANS (mental transfer), MBUILD (build thoughts), PROPEL, MOVE, GRASP, INGEST, EXPEL, SPEAK, ATTEND. Sentences with the same meaning get the same CD form: “Ram gave Sita a book” = Ram ATRANS book from Ram to Sita.

Diagrams & examples

Resolution refutation – prove mortal(Socrates):

Facts:   1. ∀x man(x) → mortal(x)         clause: ¬man(x) ∨ mortal(x)
         2. man(Socrates)                        clause: man(Socrates)
Negated goal: 3. ¬mortal(Socrates)

Resolve (1) and (3) with {x/Socrates}:   ¬man(Socrates)
Resolve (4) with (2):                    empty clause (contradiction)
=> mortal(Socrates) is proved.

Skolemization example: ∀x [ student(x) → ∃y (book(y) ∧ reads(x,y)) ] ⇒ ¬student(x) ∨ book(f(x)) and ¬student(x) ∨ reads(x, f(x)).

Important questions

Q1. Explain Skolemization with an example.

Existential quantifiers are removed by Skolem constants/functions, e.g. ∀x∃y loves(x,y) becomes loves(x,f(x)).

Q2. Explain the resolution principle and unification with an example.

Complementary literals are resolved to produce a resolvent; unification finds the MGU; prove by deriving the empty clause.

Q3. What are Horn clauses? Why are they important?

Clauses with at most one positive literal; efficient inference and the basis of Prolog.

Q4. Differentiate semantic networks and frames.

Semantic networks use graph nodes and arcs; frames group slots, defaults and procedures into structured objects, both supporting inheritance.

Q5. Explain scripts with the restaurant example.

Entry conditions, props, roles and ordered scenes: entering, ordering, eating, paying, leaving.

Q6. Explain conceptual dependency with primitive acts.

Meaning is stored as primitives like ATRANS, PTRANS, MTRANS, enabling language independence.

Quick revision

  • FOPL: constants, variables, predicates, functions, ∀, ∃.
  • Clause form: remove →, push ¬, Skolemize, drop ∀, CNF.
  • Resolution refutation: negate the goal, derive empty clause; MGU from unification.
  • Horn clause: ≤ 1 positive literal (Prolog).
  • Semantic net: nodes + is-a arcs; frame: slots + defaults + inheritance.
  • Script: stereotyped event sequence; CD: ATRANS, PTRANS, MTRANS…

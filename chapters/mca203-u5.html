Probabilistic Reasoning, Expert Systems and Learning

Overview

Real-world knowledge is often uncertain. This unit covers probability, Bayesian networks and certainty factors, then expert systems (architecture, shells, Vidwan, knowledge acquisition, MYCIN) and three basic styles of machine learning: rote, inductive and explanation-based.

Detailed notes

1. Probability theory

Probability P(A) lies in [0,1]. Rules: P(A∨B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A∧B); conditional probability P(A|B) = P(A∧B)/P(B); independence: P(A∧B) = P(A)P(B); total probability P(B) = ΣP(B|Ai)P(Ai). Probabilities express the degree of belief under uncertainty.

2. Bayes’ theorem and Bayesian networks

Bayes’ theorem: P(H|E) = P(E|H)·P(H) / P(E) – updates the prior belief in hypothesis H after evidence E. A Bayesian (belief) network is a directed acyclic graph where nodes are random variables, arcs show direct influence, and each node has a conditional probability table (CPT). The joint distribution factorises: P(x1,…,xn) = ∏ P(xi | parents(xi)). It allows reasoning (inference) under uncertainty with far fewer numbers than a full joint table. Limitation: needs many probabilities and independence assumptions.

3. Certainty factor (CF)

Used in MYCIN to handle uncertainty without full probabilities. CF = MB − MD (measure of belief minus measure of disbelief), in the range −1 (certainly false) to +1 (certainly true), 0 = unknown. Rules:

  • Rule + premise: CF(conclusion) = CF(rule) × CF(premise) (use premise only if CF > 0.2 in MYCIN).
  • AND of premises = minimum CF; OR = maximum CF.
  • Combining two rules for the same conclusion: both positive: CF1 + CF2(1 − CF1); both negative: CF1 + CF2(1 + CF1); mixed: (CF1 + CF2) / (1 − min(|CF1|, |CF2|)).

4. Expert systems and applications

An expert system is a program that captures the knowledge of a human expert in a narrow domain and reasons like one. Components:

  • Knowledge base – facts and rules (usually IF–THEN).
  • Inference engine – forward or backward chaining, conflict resolution.
  • Working memory – current facts of the case.
  • User interface and explanation facility (answers “why” and “how”).
  • Knowledge acquisition module.

Applications: medical diagnosis (MYCIN), chemical structure analysis (DENDRAL), mineral exploration (PROSPECTOR), computer configuration (XCON/R1), fault diagnosis, financial advice. Advantages: consistency, availability, preservation of expertise, explanations; limits: narrow domain, no common sense, difficult knowledge acquisition.

5. Expert system shells

A shell is an expert system with the domain knowledge removed: inference engine, user interface and knowledge-base editor are ready, so only the rules need to be added. Examples: EMYCIN (from MYCIN), CLIPS, JESS, Exsys, Vidwan.

6. Vidwan framework

Vidwan is an expert system shell developed in India at NCST (National Centre for Software Technology), Mumbai. It is rule-based, uses backward chaining with certainty factors, provides a rule editor, user-friendly interface and explanation of reasoning, and has been used for diagnostic and advisory applications. (Check your prescribed book for the exact feature list.)

7. Knowledge acquisition

The process of extracting knowledge from experts and encoding it in the knowledge base by a knowledge engineer. Techniques: interviews, questionnaires, protocol analysis (think-aloud), observation, case studies and automated learning. It is the knowledge acquisition bottleneck of expert systems – slow, costly, and experts often cannot articulate their knowledge. Steps: identification, conceptualisation, formalisation, implementation, testing.

8. Case study: MYCIN

Developed at Stanford (1970s) to diagnose bacterial blood infections and recommend antibiotic therapy. About 450+ rules, uses backward chaining from possible diagnoses, certainty factors for uncertainty, and an explanation facility. It performed at the level of specialists in evaluations but was never used clinically; its engine became EMYCIN.

9. Learning: rote learning

The system simply stores computed results or examples for later reuse (memorisation, caching), e.g. Samuel’s checkers program storing board evaluations. Trade-off: storage vs computation; needs fast retrieval.

10. Learning by induction

Generalising from specific examples to a general rule or concept: concept learning from positive and negative examples (e.g. version spaces, candidate elimination), decision-tree induction (ID3). Conclusions are plausible, not guaranteed, and depend on the inductive bias.

11. Explanation-based learning (EBL)

Learns from one example plus a domain theory: (1) explain why the example is an instance of the goal concept using the theory; (2) generalise the explanation by replacing constants with variables; (3) store the resulting general rule (operational criterion). Speeds up later problem solving and focuses learning on relevant features.

Diagrams & examples

Bayes’ theorem example. A disease affects 1% of people; the test is positive for 90% of sick people and also for 5% of healthy people. P(Disease | Positive)?

P(+) = 0.9×0.01 + 0.05×0.99 = 0.009 + 0.0495 = 0.0585
P(D|+) = 0.009 / 0.0585 = 0.154  (about 15%) – low because the disease is rare.

Certainty factor example. Rule 1 gives CF = 0.6, Rule 2 gives CF = 0.5 for the same conclusion, both positive: CF = 0.6 + 0.5×(1 − 0.6) = 0.8. A rule with CF 0.8 and premise CF 0.5: 0.8 × 0.5 = 0.4.

;; Expert-system rule (MYCIN style)
IF   the stain of the organism is gram-negative AND
     the morphology is rod AND the patient is a compromised host
THEN there is suggestive evidence (0.6) that the organism is pseudomonas

Important questions

Q1. State Bayes’ theorem and solve a simple numerical.

P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E); use the disease-test example (15.4%).

Q2. What is a Bayesian network?

A DAG of variables with CPTs; the joint probability is the product of P(x|parents).

Q3. Explain certainty factors and how CFs are combined.

CF from −1 to +1; rule×premise; and CF1 + CF2(1−CF1) for two positive rules.

Q4. Explain the architecture of an expert system.

Knowledge base, inference engine, working memory, user interface, explanation facility, knowledge acquisition.

Q5. Write a note on MYCIN.

Bacterial infection diagnosis and therapy; backward chaining, ~450 rules, certainty factors, explanations.

Q6. Explain rote learning, inductive learning and explanation-based learning.

Memorisation; generalising from examples; generalising an explanation of one example using a domain theory.

Quick revision

  • Bayes: P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E).
  • Bayesian net: DAG + CPTs; joint = ∏P(x|parents).
  • CF in [−1, 1]; combine: CF1 + CF2(1−CF1).
  • Expert system = KB + inference engine + explanation + UI; shell = ES without domain knowledge.
  • Vidwan (NCST), EMYCIN, CLIPS are shells.
  • MYCIN: bacterial infections, backward chaining, CFs.
  • Learning: rote (store), induction (generalise examples), EBL (explain + generalise).
